# Evidence base

Each entry: what was done, what was found, what you can say, what you cannot. Numbers come from the papers or their institutional press releases; where press reports disagree it is noted.

## 1. Reutskaja, Lindner, Nagel, Andersen, Camerer (2018). "Choice overload reduces neural signatures of choice set value in dorsal striatum and anterior cingulate cortex." Nature Human Behaviour 2, 925–935.

- **Design.** 19 participants in an fMRI scanner chose a landscape image to print on a T-shirt or mug from sets of 6, 12 or 24. A control condition had them browse the same sets without choosing.
- **Found.** Activity in the dorsal striatum and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) followed an inverted U over set size: highest at 12, lower at both 6 and 24. Participants also rated 12 as "the right amount". In the browsing condition the inverted U disappeared, which the authors read as the signal encoding the net value of the set (benefit of more options minus the cost of choosing).
- **Interpretation by the authors.** Past about a dozen options the cost of deciding outweighs the added value, so the value signal falls. Camerer put the practical sweet spot at roughly 8 to 15.
- **Can say.** "Brain reward and effort signals peak around 12 options and drop off past that." "The brain decides the effort is not worth it."
- **Cannot say.** "Activates the pain center." The ACC is involved in conflict monitoring, effort and pain affect, but the study measured value signals and they went *down* at 24. No threshold between 12 and 24 was tested. Nobody was observed leaving.
- Paper: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0440-2 (Caltech repository: https://authors.library.caltech.edu/90069/). Press: https://camerergroup.caltech.edu/news-blog/scientists-uncover-why-you-cant-decide-what-order-lunch-83881

## 2. Iyengar & Lepper (2000). "When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing?" Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 79(6), 995–1006.

- **Design.** Tasting booth in a grocery store alternated between 6 and 24 jams. Also lab studies with 6 vs 30 chocolates and essay topics.
- **Found.** 24 jams: about 60% of passers-by stopped, about 3% of those bought. 6 jams: about 40% stopped, about 30% bought. Roughly ten times the conversion from the smaller display.
- **Can say.** "More choice draws attention but kills the decision." "Six sold ten times better than twenty-four."
- **Caveat.** Replications are mixed; a 2010 meta-analysis (Scheibehenne et al.) found the average effect near zero across studies, with strong effects only under some conditions (no prior preference, hard-to-compare options, time pressure). Use the jam study as the vivid example, not as a universal law.

## 3. Hick's law (Hick 1952, Hyman 1953)

- Reaction time to choose rises with the logarithm of the number of equally likely options: RT = a + b·log2(n+1).
- **Can say.** "Every doubling of the options adds a fixed chunk of decision time." Applies to simple, well-learned choices; for complex options the growth is worse than logarithmic.

## 4. Working-memory limits

- **Miller (1956)**, "The magical number seven, plus or minus two." Span for simple items such as digits.
- **Cowan (2001)**, Behavioral and Brain Sciences: when chunking and rehearsal are prevented, the capacity is about 4 chunks.
- **Can say.** "People hold about four things in mind at once; seven is the ceiling for the simplest items."
- **Cannot say.** "The brain can only see seven things." Miller's number is about memory span, not perception.

## 5. Cognitive load theory and multimedia principles

- **Sweller (1988)** and later: learning fails when extraneous load (how material is presented) eats working memory needed for the actual content.
- **Mayer**, multimedia learning principles. Coherence principle: people learn better when extraneous words, pictures and sounds are excluded. Redundancy and split-attention principles point the same way.
- **Can say.** "Decoration is not free. Every extra element is paid for out of the attention budget for the thing you want understood."

## 6. Clutter competes for attention

- **Kastner et al. (1998)** Science; **McMains & Kastner (2011)** Journal of Neuroscience, "Interactions of top-down and bottom-up mechanisms in human visual cortex." Objects presented together suppress each other's representation in visual cortex (biased competition). Attention can rescue the target, but the competition is still there and costs effort.
- **Can say.** "Every object on the screen is competing with the one you want people to look at." "Clutter tires people out over time" (Kastner's own phrasing in Princeton Alumni Weekly, https://paw.princeton.edu/article/psychology-your-attention-please).
- **Cannot say.** Anything about pain, stress hormones or emotion from this work. It is visual cortex only.

## 7. First impressions and visual complexity

- **Lindgaard et al. (2006)**, Behaviour & Information Technology: people form a stable judgement of a web page's appeal in about 50 ms.
- **Reinecke et al. (2013)**, CHI, "Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics": visual complexity and colourfulness predict appeal; low-to-medium complexity scores highest, and the effect holds across demographics.
- **Tuch et al. (2009/2012)**: higher visual complexity of web pages increases physiological arousal and lowers rated pleasantness and performance.
- **Can say.** "People have judged your page before they have read a word, and what they are judging is how busy it looks."

## Adjacent studies people confuse with the above

- **Lyons & Beilock (2012)**, PLOS ONE, "When math hurts." High math-anxious people show posterior insula (pain-related) activity when *anticipating* math. About anxiety, not element count. This is the real source of the "brain feels pain" headline.
- **Tolin et al. (2012)**, Archives of General Psychiatry. Hoarders deciding to discard their own possessions show abnormal ACC and insula activity. Clinical group, about discarding, not viewing.
- **Eisenberger et al. (2003)**, Science. Social rejection activates the dorsal ACC, which is where "the ACC is the pain centre" shorthand came from.
- **Visual stress / pattern glare** (Wilkins and colleagues; 2026 review "A Cerebral Basis for Visual Discomfort and Visual Stress", Vision, https://doi.org/10.3390/vision10020034). Striped, high-contrast patterns overdrive visual cortex and cause discomfort or headache in susceptible people. About pattern structure, not number of elements.

## One-paragraph version for a pitch

People judge a page in about a twentieth of a second, and what they judge is how busy it looks. In the classic shop experiment, a table with 24 jams drew a crowd and sold a tenth as much as a table with 6. Brain scans show why: the brain's reward signal for a set of options peaks at around a dozen and switches off past that, because the effort of choosing has outgrown the value of the choice. And every extra element on a screen competes for the same attention as the one thing you need people to see. Fewer things, chosen on purpose, is not taste. It is how the audience works.
